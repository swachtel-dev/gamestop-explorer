How Stock Trading Works (And How Short Selling Flips It)

Buying a Stock: The Normal Way to Invest

When you buy a stock, you are buying a small ownership stake in a company. If you buy 10 shares of GameStop at $20 per share, you spend $200. You now own those shares. If the price later rises to $50, you can sell for $500, making a $300 profit. If the price falls to $5, you can sell for $50, losing $150. The most you can ever lose is what you paid: $200. Your shares cannot go below $0.

This is called a long position: you buy first, hope the price goes up, and sell later. Most investors are long most of the time.

Short Selling: Betting a Stock Will Fall

Short selling is the opposite. Instead of buying a stock and hoping it goes up, a short seller bets the stock will go down. The mechanics are unusual. You cannot sell something you do not own, so a short seller has to borrow shares from someone who does own them, sell those borrowed shares on the open market, and then buy shares back later to return to the lender.

If the price falls between the time you sell and the time you buy back, you profit. If the price rises, you lose money. And unlike owning a stock (where the worst case is $0), a stock's price can rise without limit, which means short selling losses are theoretically unlimited.

Key Terms So Far

  • Long position: Buying a stock and holding it, hoping the price rises. Your maximum loss is what you paid.
  • Short position: Borrowing and selling a stock you do not own, hoping to buy it back cheaper. Your potential loss is unlimited.
  • Covering: When a short seller buys shares to return to the lender, closing out their position. If the price has risen, covering means buying at a loss.
  • Margin call: When your broker demands you deposit more cash because your losses are growing. If you cannot meet the margin call, the broker can force you to close your position immediately.
  • Short interest: The total number of shares currently sold short, usually shown as a percentage of the float (the shares available to be publicly traded). If a company has 10 million shares in its float and 2 million are sold short, short interest is 20%.

Why GameStop's Short Interest Was Extraordinary

Normal short interest on a stock is 2-5%. Above 20% is considered high. GameStop's short interest in January 2021 was approximately 140%. That means more shares had been sold short than actually existed in the float.

How is that possible? Because the same share can be borrowed and sold more than once. Here is a simplified example:

  1. Investor A owns 100 shares and lends them to Short Seller B.
  2. Short Seller B sells those 100 shares on the open market. Investor C buys them.
  3. Investor C now owns those 100 shares. C's broker lends them to Short Seller D.
  4. Short Seller D sells those 100 shares on the open market.

The original 100 shares have now been sold short twice. Short interest on those shares is 200%. This re-lending of borrowed shares is legal and common. But at extreme levels it creates a dangerous situation: if Short Seller B and Short Seller D both need to buy shares at the same time to close their positions, they need 200 shares, but only 100 actually exist in circulation. They are competing with each other for a limited supply, which drives the price up.

The short squeeze feedback loop When short interest is very high and the price starts rising, short sellers start losing money. Their brokers issue margin calls, forcing them to either add cash or close their positions by buying shares. But their buying pushes the price up further, which triggers margin calls for other short sellers, who also have to buy, which pushes the price up more. Each round of forced buying triggers the next round. This self-reinforcing cycle is called a short squeeze. The higher the short interest, the more violent the squeeze.

One More Amplifier: The Options Effect

There is one more piece of the GameStop story you need to understand, and it comes from options trading. You do not need to understand options in depth, but you need to know one thing about how they work.

A call option is a contract that gives you the right to buy a stock at a set price. If you buy a call option on GameStop with a $50 price, and GameStop rises to $300, you can buy at $50 and immediately have a share worth $300. Call options are a way to bet that a stock will rise.

Here is the important part: when someone sells a call option, the seller takes on risk. To manage that risk, the seller (usually a large financial firm called a market maker) buys some shares of the stock as a hedge. As the stock price rises, the market maker has to buy more shares to stay hedged. This creates a second feedback loop:

  1. Retail investors buy call options, betting GameStop will rise.
  2. Market makers sell those options and hedge by buying GameStop shares.
  3. Their buying pushes the price up.
  4. The higher price means market makers need to buy even more shares to stay hedged.
  5. More buying pushes the price up further, and the cycle repeats.

This is called a gamma ramp. In the GameStop squeeze, the gamma ramp was running simultaneously with the short squeeze, creating two feedback loops at once: short sellers being forced to buy AND market makers being forced to buy. The combination is what drove the stock from $20 to nearly $500 in three weeks.

The Squeeze: What Happened and What Could Have

The GameStop squeeze was not random. It was the product of specific conditions: extremely high short interest (140% of the float sold short), coordinated retail buying on r/WallStreetBets, an options-driven feedback loop (the gamma ramp from the previous page), and ultimately, broker intervention that cut off buying. Change any one of these and the outcome changes dramatically.

Who Did What: A Factual Timeline

The GameStop squeeze involved several groups of actors whose actions are a matter of public record. This timeline presents what each actor did and when, without characterizing their motivations. The interpretation is yours to make.

The Key Actors

  • Melvin Capital: Hedge fund with a large short position in GameStop. Managed approximately $12.5 billion in assets.
  • Citron Research: Short-selling research firm run by Andrew Left. Published reports arguing that specific stocks were overvalued (bearish reports), hoping to profit when those stocks declined.
  • r/WallStreetBets (WSB): Reddit community with approximately 2 million members in early January 2021 (growing to 9+ million by month's end). Known for high-risk trading and irreverent culture.
  • Robinhood: Commission-free trading platform with approximately 13 million monthly active users. Robinhood does not charge users trading fees. Instead, it makes money through "payment for order flow" (PFOF): when you place a trade on Robinhood, Robinhood routes your order to a large market-making firm (like Citadel Securities), which pays Robinhood for the right to execute that trade.
  • Citadel Securities: One of the largest market makers in U.S. equities. A market maker is a firm that stands ready to buy or sell a stock at any time, earning a small profit on each transaction. Citadel Securities executed approximately 47% of all U.S. retail stock trades. Separately, Citadel LLC (a hedge fund under the same parent company) invested $2 billion in Melvin Capital during the squeeze.
  • DTCC (Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation): The central organization that processes and settles U.S. stock trades. When you buy a stock, the trade does not settle instantly; the DTCC handles the transfer of shares and cash between buyer and seller over a period of days. During that settlement period, the DTCC requires brokers to post cash deposits as a guarantee that the trade will complete.
  • SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission): Federal regulator overseeing securities markets.

The Regulatory Aftermath

The GameStop squeeze triggered intense regulatory debate. Multiple congressional hearings were held, the SEC published a staff report, and various rule changes were proposed. The landscape below summarizes what rules existed before the squeeze, what was proposed afterward, and what actually changed.

Rules That Existed

  • Regulation SHO (2005): Requires brokers to locate shares before executing a short sale. Contains a "close-out" requirement for failures to deliver. Includes an exemption for bona fide market makers.
  • Short Interest Reporting: FINRA requires firms to report short positions twice monthly (settlement dates of the 15th and last business day). Data is published with an 11-day lag. No real-time or daily reporting requirement exists.
  • Payment for Order Flow (PFOF): Legal under SEC rules. Broker-dealers can route retail orders to market makers in exchange for payment, provided they meet "best execution" obligations.
  • Social Media and Market Manipulation: SEC Rule 10b-5 prohibits fraud and manipulation. No specific rules address coordination via social media forums. The line between "sharing investment ideas" and "coordinating a pump" is not clearly defined in existing regulation.
  • Clearing and Collateral: The DTCC sets collateral requirements for clearing members. During periods of high volatility, margin requirements can increase dramatically. There is no cap on how much or how quickly these requirements can change.

What Was Proposed After the Squeeze

  • Real-time short interest disclosure: Multiple proposals to require daily or real-time reporting of short positions, replacing the twice-monthly, 11-day-lag system. Supporters argue transparency would reduce the information asymmetry between institutional shorts and retail investors. Opponents argue real-time disclosure would expose trading strategies and reduce the incentive to short, which serves a legitimate price-discovery function.
  • PFOF ban or reform: SEC Chair Gary Gensler expressed concern about conflicts of interest in payment for order flow. The SEC proposed rules requiring more disclosure about order execution quality. A full ban was discussed but not enacted. Some argued PFOF subsidizes commission-free trading; removing it would reimpose trading costs on retail investors.
  • T+1 settlement: Reducing the settlement period from T+2 (trade date plus two business days) to T+1. Faster settlement reduces the collateral brokers must post with clearinghouses, which could prevent the liquidity crunch that led to buy restrictions. This was the most broadly supported reform.
  • Gamification restrictions: Proposals to regulate app design features that encourage frequent trading (confetti animations, push notifications about trending stocks). Limited traction; First Amendment and definitional challenges.
  • Social media monitoring: Some legislators proposed SEC monitoring of social media for coordinated manipulation. Civil liberties groups and the SEC itself expressed concerns about feasibility and free speech implications.

What Actually Changed

The short answer: not much Most proposals stalled. The one significant structural change was the move to T+1 settlement, which the SEC finalized in February 2023 and implemented in May 2024. PFOF remains legal. Short interest reporting is still twice-monthly. No new rules specifically address social media coordination. The SEC staff report (October 2021) concluded that the squeeze was driven by positive sentiment, not a short squeeze in the traditional sense, and did not recommend major enforcement actions.

The SEC's October 2021 staff report on the GameStop events made several observations:

  • GME short interest exceeded 100% of the public float, which is legal but creates fragility.
  • The buying pressure was primarily driven by retail investors, not institutional short covering (though short covering contributed to the volume).
  • Broker restrictions on buying were driven by clearing deposit requirements, not by a desire to protect short sellers.
  • The report did not find evidence of market manipulation by retail investors or collusion between Robinhood and Citadel.
  • The report identified structural concerns (concentrated short interest, clearing system fragility, PFOF conflicts) but stopped short of proposing specific rule changes.

The Open Questions

The GameStop squeeze raised questions that remain unresolved:

  • Should short interest above a certain threshold (50%? 100%?) trigger mandatory disclosure or position limits?
  • When does sharing investment ideas in a public forum become coordinated market manipulation?
  • Should brokers be allowed to restrict buying (but not selling) during volatility, and if so, under what conditions?
  • Does payment for order flow create an inherent conflict between a broker's fiduciary duty to clients and its revenue model?
  • Is the current clearing infrastructure adequate for the volume and speed of modern retail trading?
